The fourMs Lab, for Music, Mind, Motion and Machines, is a lab at the University of Oslo for artistic and scientific research on music, mind, motion and machines, and a national research infrastructure. It is owned by the Department of Musicology and is the central lab of RITMO, the Centre for Interdisciplinary Studies in Rhythm, Time and Motion. The talk follows a few questions: why, what, how and who, then MusicLab and the standstill championship, teaching, history, and what comes next, under When?. Right moves on, down goes deeper.

Why?

Why a lab for music and motion?

First question: why a music department needs a motion lab.

Music is not only sound. It is also motion.

Music is made by bodies and experienced by bodies: the actions that produce sound, the gestures that accompany it, the motion of listeners and dancers. To study music, we need to measure that motion, and the physiology that goes with it, as precisely as we measure sound.

The name says what the lab is for: music, mind, motion and machines. The lab started as a collaboration between musicology and informatics, and that collaboration grew into RITMO in 2017, when psychology joined. Today it is used for music-related body motion, performance and music psychology, and increasingly for linguistics, dance, sports and well-being.

The lab's web pages call it a world-class research infrastructure for studies of human body movement and physiology in an immersive multimedia environment. That is true but too narrow. The lab is as much a place for artistic research and performance, for spatial audio with a 24-channel loudspeaker rig and wavefield synthesis, for VR and interactive systems, as for measuring bodies. It spans, in the words of its 2022 description, from the arts and humanities to the social and natural sciences. It is a national research infrastructure recognised by the Research Council of Norway since , a UiO:Life Science core facility, and open to external users, who can rent it.

A large audio, video and control patch connects the lab and the control room. The lab is next door to the cognitive labs of the Department of Psychology.

The strength of the lab is that everything can be recorded together and in sync: optical motion capture, eye tracking, physiology, multichannel audio and video. Here the recording is followed live on the screens of the control room.

The black box is rebuilt for each study: a forest of cameras around a pianist one day, a violinist and a singer the next.

Optical and inertial motion capture with sub-millimetre precision, up to frames per second; sensors for muscle activity, heart rate and breathing; eye trackers for pupillometry; and audio and video: -channel audio recording, a -loudspeaker array, video, lighting and instruments. Data can be exported in standard formats such as C3D, CSV and BVH.

In the lab

The fixed installation in the lab, as listed on the lab's web pages: optical, inertial and magnetic motion capture, biosignal sensors, a multichannel loudspeaker rig and a wavefield synthesis array, and a set of new keyboard instruments.

Portable

The portable equipment can be used in the lab or taken out: a second motion capture system and suits, eye trackers, wearable sensors for whole groups, video and lighting, and sound recording.

Infrared cameras on the truss, reflective markers by the hundred, and a suit with markers that the software turns into a moving skeleton in real time.

Much of the equipment travels. With wireless sensors for whole groups, the lab can go to concert halls, schools and festivals and record musicians and audiences where the music happens; MusicLab relies mainly on this mobile equipment. The data go to secure university storage and can be analysed on GPU-accelerated remote desktops.

Open data

The lab handbook is open online. Data are moved to open formats, following a "good enough practice" approach rather than waiting for perfection, and openly licensed where possible. The barriers are real: privacy, because bodies and faces identify people, and copyright, because music is protected. The lab also publishes methods papers comparing motion capture systems.

Despite the name, MusicLab is not a physical lab. It is an open research innovation project between RITMO and the University Library. It uses resources from the fourMs Lab, primarily the mobile equipment, which goes out to real venues: concert halls, clubs, libraries and festivals, where the audience is part of the event. Everything about it is on musiclab.uio.no.

A concert, data collection and a conversation with the audience, in one event

Always free and open to everyone, and always streamed

MusicLab is an open research innovation project between RITMO and the University Library, exploring new ways to collect and communicate music-related research data. Each event combines a concert, a data collection and a conversation with the audience, often with "data jockeys" analysing the data live on stage. In the project's own words, MusicLab is always free and open to everyone, and all events are streamed.

Every MusicLab is built around a concert, with four other parts around it: a workshop, data collection during the concert, data jockeying, where researchers analyse the fresh data live on stage, and a panel discussion with the musicians and researchers. The audience sees the whole research process, from data collection to the first results.

Standstill

The Norwegian Championship of Standstill

Of everything RITMO did, this is what reached the media most often.

How still can you stand?

Even when we try to stand completely still, we move: we breathe, our heart beats, we sway to keep our balance. The Norwegian Championship of Standstill asked people to stand as still as they could while their motion was measured with motion capture, in silence and with music. Music makes it harder: we move involuntarily, even when we try not to, and how much depends on the music.

The championship

The championship was held every year with hundreds of participants. It was both a way of collecting data on micromotion and a way of reaching the public, and it was covered by NRK's evening news in 2015.

The Oslo Standstill Database gathers motion capture recordings of people trying to stand still, in silence and with music: recordings in collections, of them from the championships, from an artistic project in to recordings in . The collections are published openly, each with its own DOI, and they are the data behind the book Still Standing.

When?

From Musical Gestures in 2004 to HumanLab in 2027

Sixth question: how the lab came to be.
The work began with the Musical Gestures project in 2004, with the first recordings in a borrowed video studio, and the first dedicated lab followed in 2006. In 2008, the Sensing Music-related Actions project made it a collaboration between musicology and informatics; the lab got its name and its first motion capture systems, and became a national infrastructure, recognised by the Research Council of Norway. That collaboration grew into RITMO in 2017, and since 2018 the lab has been in custom-built rooms as RITMO's central lab. The fourMs Lab Upgrade brought infrastructure funding from the Research Council from 2022 to 2031, and in 2026 the lab became a HumSam core facility at UiO.

Three labs ago

From a borrowed blue-screen studio, through a lab with a loudspeaker truss, to a converted seminar room painted black: each move brought more space and better equipment.

HumanLab, summer 2027

A new laboratory at the University of Oslo, run by musicology and linguistics together

From the summer of 2027, the fourMs Lab becomes part of HumanLab, a new laboratory at the University of Oslo run jointly by the Department of Musicology and the Department of Linguistics and Scandinavian Studies. The fourMs Lab continues there as a national research infrastructure. It brings the fourMs Lab together with the linguists' socio-cognitive laboratory, so that sound, body and mind can be measured together, from motion capture and physiology to brain activity and eye tracking.

The lab is older than the centres that use it, and it will outlive them. It began with the Musical Gestures project in 2004 and became a national infrastructure in 2008. The collaboration in the lab grew into RITMO, a Centre of Excellence for rhythm, time and motion from 2017, with the lab as its central facility. MishMash, the national Centre for AI and Creativity from 2025, builds on the same environment. And from 2027, the lab continues as a national infrastructure within HumanLab, owned by the Department of Musicology and the Department of Linguistics and Scandinavian Studies, so that it no longer depends on a single centre.
